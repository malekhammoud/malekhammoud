## The problem

Cleanup effort gets allocated by intuition. A group decides a park needs
attention, turns up, and covers the part of it they can walk. Nobody has a map
of where the litter actually is, because producing one by hand costs more
person-hours than the cleanup does.

## The constraint

Aerial detection is a genuinely harder computer vision problem than
ground-level detection, and the constraints stack up fast:

- **The objects are small and the camera is far away.** A bottle occupies a
  handful of pixels from altitude.
- **The background is the hard part.** Litter against grass, gravel, leaf
  litter and shadow all look different, and shadows move during the flight.
- **Compute is on the airframe.** A Raspberry Pi with a flight controller
  already doing real-time work is not a generous inference budget.
- **A detection is useless without a position.** Finding something and not
  knowing where it was is the same as not finding it.

## What I built

The airframe pairs a **Pixhawk** flight controller with a **Raspberry Pi** as
the companion computer — the Pixhawk flies, the Pi thinks. They talk over
MAVLink, which is what makes the last constraint tractable: the Pi can ask the
flight controller where the aircraft was at the moment a frame was captured.

**Detection** runs over frames from the downward camera, tuned for small
objects against noisy natural backgrounds rather than for the well-lit,
centre-framed objects most models are trained on.

**Geolocation** is the piece that turns detections into something usable.
Each detection is tagged with the aircraft's position and attitude at capture
time and projected to a ground coordinate, so the output is a map of
locations rather than a folder of photographs.

## The outcome

A working proof of concept: the aircraft flies a survey pattern, detects litter
from altitude, and produces a geolocated map of what it found — on a hardware
budget of hobby components rather than survey equipment.

It's honestly labelled a proof of concept. It demonstrates the pipeline end to
end; it has not been flown at the scale or across the range of conditions that
would justify calling it reliable.

## Why it's on this page

Because the pattern generalises further than litter. "Fly a cheap sensor over a
large area, detect a specific thing, and hand back coordinates" is the same
system whether the thing is rubbish, crop damage, roof condition or missing
inventory. The interesting engineering is the geolocation and the small-object
detection, and both transfer.
