## The problem

Herbicide is applied to fields, not to weeds. A sprayer covers everything
because it has no way to tell the difference, which means the overwhelming
majority of what gets sprayed lands on soil and crop rather than on the plant
it was meant for.

The technology to do better exists — it just usually costs more than a small
farm has.

## The constraint

The interesting constraint was cost, and it drove every other decision.
Commercial precision-spraying rigs are priced for industrial agriculture. A
system that only works with expensive sensors solves the problem for people who
already have options.

That ruled out most of the obvious answers and left a harder one: a single
camera, a cheap compute board, and a mechanism I could fabricate myself.

Two more constraints came from the environment rather than the budget:

- **A field is not a lab.** Lighting changes hour to hour, plants move in the
  wind, and the ground is uneven enough to shift the camera between frames.
- **The robot has to not drive into things**, including things that were not
  there when it started.

## What I built

**Detection** runs on a Raspberry Pi using OpenCV — classifying what's in front
of the machine as crop, weed or ground, frame by frame, on hardware with no
GPU. That last part is the real constraint: it forces you to spend your compute
budget deliberately rather than throwing a large model at the problem.

**The spray assembly** is 3D-printed and designed around a single idea: the
nozzle has to arrive over a specific plant, not a general area. Designing it
myself in CAD meant I could iterate the geometry against what detection could
actually resolve, rather than fitting the software to a part I'd bought.

**Navigation and obstacle avoidance** keep it driving a row without hitting
anything, which sounds like the simple part and is not. Most of the failure
modes I found in testing were navigation failures, not detection failures.

## The outcome

It works: the robot drives a row, identifies weeds and sprays them
individually, at a hardware cost far below a commercial system.

The honest framing is that it's a proven prototype rather than a product. It
demonstrated the approach well enough to take bronze at the **Canada-Wide
Science Fair** in 2024 and gold at the regional fair, and the detection and
mechanism both do what they claim. Turning it into something a farm could rely
on for a season is a different and much larger piece of work.

## Why it's on this page

It's the clearest example of the thing I'm usually hired for now: taking a
capability that only works when it's expensive, and finding the version of it
that works on hardware someone can actually own. That's the same argument as
running your own models — different domain, identical shape.
