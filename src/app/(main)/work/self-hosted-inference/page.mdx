## The problem

Every request to a hosted model is a metered event. That's fine while you're
experimenting and it stops being fine the moment something works — the bill
scales with success, and the thing you built becomes more expensive precisely
as it becomes more useful.

The second problem is quieter and usually matters more. Sending a prompt to a
third-party API means sending whatever is in that prompt. For a lot of
organisations that is the end of the conversation: the data is under a client
contract, a regulator, or a policy that predates anyone having an opinion about
language models.

## The constraint

Running your own model is easy to demo and hard to depend on. A script that
loads weights and answers one question proves nothing. The constraints that
actually shape the design are:

- **It has to survive a crash.** Inference processes die. If a dead process
  means a dead product, you haven't built a service, you've built a demo.
- **It has to authenticate.** An open port serving a language model on your
  network is a liability, not a feature.
- **It has to handle more than one model.** Different jobs want different
  weights, and reloading a model per request costs more than the request.
- **It has to run on hardware that exists.** Not a rented H100 cluster — a
  machine you can buy once and put on a shelf.

## What I built

The host is an **M4 Mac Mini**. Apple Silicon is an unusual choice and a
deliberate one: unified memory means the GPU addresses the same pool as the
CPU, so a machine with a sane amount of RAM can hold a model that would
otherwise need a discrete card, at a fraction of the power draw and none of the
noise. It sits on a shelf and costs what it costs, once.

Serving is **MLX-LM**, Apple's array framework, running quantised open-weight
**Qwen** models. Quantisation is the lever that decides what fits: it trades a
small amount of quality for a large amount of memory, and getting that trade
right for the specific job is most of the work in sizing a deployment.

In front of it is a **router I wrote in Python**, which is where the
service-shaped behaviour lives:

- **Authentication** on every request, so the model is not simply reachable.
- **Model management** — weights stay resident between requests, and switching
  models is an explicit operation rather than an accident.
- **Crash recovery** — the supervisor notices a dead worker and brings it back
  without human involvement.
- **Request routing**, so different callers can be pointed at different models
  without every client needing to know the topology.

None of that is exotic. It is the difference between a script and something a
team can put behind a product.

## The outcome

The cost model inverts. Instead of a per-token line that grows with usage,
there's a one-off hardware cost and an electricity bill — and the marginal cost
of the ten-thousandth request is approximately zero. Where the crossover point
sits depends entirely on your volume, which is why I'd rather work it out with
you against real numbers than quote you a saving.

The part that's harder to put a figure on: the data never leaves. There is no
vendor to add to a security questionnaire, no region to worry about, and no
policy change to read on someone else's blog.

<Callout title="Worth saying plainly">
  Self-hosting is not automatically cheaper. Below a certain volume an API is
  the correct answer and I will tell you so. The argument for owning the stack
  is strongest when you have steady throughput, a data constraint, or both.
</Callout>

## What I'd reuse

The router is the reusable part. The model, the quantisation and the hardware
are all sizing decisions that should be made against your workload — but
auth, supervision, model management and routing are the same problem every
time, and they are the parts most self-hosting attempts skip and then regret.
